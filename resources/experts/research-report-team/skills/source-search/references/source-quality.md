# Source Quality Reference

Place every source in one tier before relying on it. A tier states how many independent sources a claim needs; tier 4 sources never support a claim in the report.

## Tier 1 — Primary data and official records
Raw statistics, datasets, filings, regulations, court records, standards documents, full-text peer-reviewed studies, official transcripts, first-party reports from the responsible body. Record the exact figure or quote with its location, and cite the body itself.

## Tier 2 — Peer-reviewed and major outlets
Established news organizations and specialist trade press that name their sources; peer-reviewed work consulted only through its abstract. Usable for a claim, but move the citation to the primary source whenever the underlying document is reachable.

## Tier 3 — Reputable secondary
Industry surveys, NGO or advocacy analysis, vendor documentation, expert commentary, established encyclopedias. Good for context and leads; a claim carried only by tier 3 sources needs one more independent source from a different tier.

## Tier 4 — Unverified
Authorless blogs, content aggregators, anonymous statistics, social posts, reprinted press material with no original reporting. Never cited in the report; use only as a pointer toward a better source.

## Red flags
- No publication date and no visible revision note.
- Circular citations: several outlets all tracing back to one unreachable origin.
- Press-release-only evidence: nominally separate sources that are all the same release.
- Anonymous statistics ("according to sources") with no named author or method.
- Numbers with no unit, no base year or no denominator.
- A "study" that is a marketing claim wearing a chart.

## Paywalls
1. If the title, date and abstract are visible, cite the source normally.
2. Tag the entry `abstract only` and quote only the visible text.
3. Never predict what the full text probably says; the claim it would support keeps only what the visible text shows, and the lead is told the note.
