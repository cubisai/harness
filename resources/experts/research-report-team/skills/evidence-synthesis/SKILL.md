# Evidence Synthesis

## When to use
Use after the researcher's sourced evidence is in and before fact-checking runs, when raw findings must become answers the lead can use.

## Step 1 — Group by sub-question
1. Bucket every sourced item under its sub-question. Assumed items go in a separate list; they enter no reasoning.
2. Within each bucket, mark what agrees, what conflicts, and what answers an aspect the sub-question never asked.

## Step 2 — Find the pattern
3. Name one of four shapes per bucket: pattern (sources cluster on one value or direction), trend (a change over time), split (two credible readings by region, segment or method), outlier (one source far from the rest).
4. Write one line per bucket: the shape, the value or direction, and the count of independent sources behind it.
5. Do not average a conflict into one number; state the split with the sources on each side.

## Step 3 — Adversarial check
6. Take the leading answer and list the evidence that would or does disconfirm it.
7. Test whether the strongest case for the opposite reading survives its citations. If it does, the honest result is a split, not a winner — say so with the sources on each side.
8. Name the specific missing evidence that would settle the question (a dataset, a study year, a region); it belongs in open questions, not in the prose.

## Step 4 — State the answer
9. One sentence per sub-question: the finding, its strength, and the independent source count.
   - strong — multiple independent sources, largely primary or official, conflicts resolved.
   - moderate — agreement with one unresolved caveat or thin area.
   - tentative — a pattern visible but not independently confirmed.
   Form: "Strong (6 sources): <the finding>. Moderate (3 sources): <the finding>, except <the area>."
10. Under each answer, list its assumptions (what had to hold but was not researched) and what would change the conclusion (the concrete evidence that could reverse it).
11. Keep the `[n]` markers: every strength and count points at the sourced items behind it.

## Handoff
12. Deliver to the lead: the one-sentence answers with strength and counts, the alternative readings set aside and why, and the assumptions — the fact-checker needs the claims exactly as they will read.

## Do not
- Do not smooth a split into a single value; whatever you hand down becomes the report.
- Do not read sources the researcher never gathered; if the set is thin, say tentative and name what is missing.
- Do not answer the parent question directly; deliver one answer per sub-question and let the lead combine them.
- Do not restate the full source text in the analysis; the count and the [n] pointer are enough.
- Do not let the answer's strength lean on wording; the count behind it does the work.
- Do not turn assumed items into sourced ones; hand them back labeled assumed.

## Output
- Write the full result (grouped evidence, per-sub-question shapes and conflicts, answers with strength and counts, assumptions, what would change the conclusion) to a Markdown file in the team workspace or the project, at the path given in the brief from the lead.
- Reply to the lead with at most 200 words plus that file path and any open questions.
